## K1 — sealed mechanistic report

MECHANISTIC REPORT

Scope and experiment design

The campaign comprised 12 completed aqueous batches. Every batch used water and Catalyst A, with a 2 × 2 × 3 design: reagent loading was 0.005 or 0.035 mol, water volume was 0.040 or 0.080 L, and Catalyst A loading was 0.0005, 0.0025, or 0.0050 mol. Thus, the nominal reagent concentration ranged from 0.0625 to 0.875 mol L−1. Each batch received one intermediate pH-meter measurement and one terminal final assay. No heating, deliberate waiting-time variation, solvent comparison, catalyst-identity comparison, or catalyst-free control was performed.

The corresponding mean precipitation proxies were 0.006927, 0.000638, 0.059736, and 0.032149. High reagent loading strongly increased precipitation at both volumes. At high loading, doubling volume reduced the mean proxy by 0.02759; at low loading, the already small proxy declined by 0.00629. This is consistent with precipitation being driven mainly by concentration or ionic product rather than by total reagent amount alone.

The final-assay acid-fraction means for the same four cells were 0.000302, 0.008637, 0.003095, and 0.000000. These values are close to the declared instrumental noise and several were clipped at zero. Consequently, only the low-loading dilution comparison provides a resolved directional signal: batches 4–6 had fractions of 0.00691–0.01029, compared with 0.00017–0.00042 in batches 1–3. The high-loading results do not reproduce that direction. They should not be forced into a precise monotonic law.

How the observations changed the account

Batches 1–3 versus 4–6 established the low-loading dilution response: dilution raised normalized pH and apparently raised the free dissociation fraction while leaving precipitation near its floor. Batches 7–9 established the concentrated boundary: they had the lowest normalized pH and the largest precipitation signals. Batch 7, the sealed anchor, gave normalized pH 0.096195 and precipitation 0.068974. Batches 10–12 then showed that dilution of the same 0.035 mol loading raised normalized pH to about 0.131 and reduced precipitation to about 0.027–0.035.

The catalyst-loading sweeps caused me to reject a simple claim that Catalyst A amount controls any channel monotonically. For example, at high loading and 0.040 L, precipitation was 0.068974, 0.051610, and 0.058623 as catalyst increased; at 0.080 L it was 0.034194, 0.035190, and 0.027063. These changes are nonmonotonic and small relative to the clear reagent and volume effects. Catalyst loading may be weakly coupled, saturated, or merely correlated with an unresolved ionic component, but this study does not identify its role.

The intermediate pH-meter results also revised the evidential weighting. Normalized pH generally agreed reasonably with the final assay—for example, batch 7 gave 0.098711 by pH meter and 0.096195 finally—but its secondary channels were less stable. In batch 9 the pH meter reported acid fraction 0.038502 whereas the final assay gave 0.004920; in batch 4 it reported precipitation 0.048799 whereas the final assay gave zero. I therefore treat intermediate secondary-channel excursions as measurement variability rather than as evidence for transient species.

Plausible common equilibrium network

A parsimonious network capable of coupling all three public channels is:

HA(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + A−(aq)
M+(aq) + A−(aq) ⇌ MA(aq)
M+(aq) + A−(aq) ⇌ MA(s)

Here HA is the anonymous weak acid or acid-bearing reagent, A− is its free dissociated form, M+ is an anonymous counter-species, MA(aq) is an optional dissolved ion pair or complex, and MA(s) is a precipitated phase. I do not identify M+ with Catalyst A as an observed fact. That is one possible interpretation, but catalyst identity and free-M concentration were not measured.

Using activities, suitable equilibrium relations are

Ka = a(H+)a(A−)/a(HA),
Kpair = a(MA)/(a(M+)a(A−)),
and, when solid is present, Ksp = a(M+)a(A−).

Representative acid-family and M-family balances are

nA,total = V([HA] + [A−] + [MA]aq) + nMA(s),
nM,total = V([M+] + [MA]aq) + nMA(s).

Electroneutrality additionally requires positive charge, including H+ and M+, to balance A−, OH−, and any unobserved spectator ions. Because spectator composition and activity coefficients were not measured, concentration-form equilibrium constants cannot be recovered uniquely.

The normalized-pH channel follows

pHnormalized = −log10(a(H+))/14.

A natural definition of the free dissociation fraction is approximately

ffree = n(A−,free)/nA,total,

although the exact environmental mapping of the public fraction was not independently calibrated here. The precipitation channel can be represented as a bounded monotone sensor function

P = g(nMA(s), particle state, matrix),  0 ≤ P ≤ 1,

rather than equating its numerical value directly with a mole fraction.

This network explains the major coupling qualitatively. Greater acid loading raises hydrogen activity and lowers normalized pH. Greater concentration also raises the M+/A− ionic product and makes the solid branch more favorable, increasing the precipitation proxy. Dilution reduces hydrogen activity, raises normalized pH, and reduces the precipitation-driving ionic product. Removal of free A− through pairing or precipitation can, in turn, shift acid dissociation and couple the fraction channel to the other two. Thus one equilibrium network can parsimoniously explain all three channels.

Topology claim versus parameter refitting

The defensible topology claim is limited: the data are compatible with an acid-dissociation node coupled to an A−-removal branch that can generate a solid. The strong, jointly structured pH and precipitation responses support that broad topology.

The data do not uniquely establish the distinct aqueous MA intermediate. A model with direct precipitation M+ + A− ⇌ MA(s), but no stable MA(aq), could reproduce the principal trends after refitting Ka, Ksp, activity coefficients, and the precipitation-sensor mapping. Conversely, including MA(aq) adds a buffering reservoir that could help reproduce the small and noisy free-fraction values. Changing Ka, Kpair, Ksp, or sensor scale within the supplied three-node topology is parameter refitting; adding or removing MA(aq), invoking a second acid, or replacing equilibrium precipitation with aggregation kinetics is a topology change. This campaign discriminates concentration-sensitive precipitation from no coupling, but it does not discriminate those finer topologies.

Dossier and prior assessment

No task-specific instance property dossier or numerical instance prior was supplied: the material-information dossier was explicitly null. A qualitative structural prior was supplied separately, proposing HA dissociation, a distinct aqueous MA intermediate, and MA(s) formation. The observations support the HA/acid-response and solid-formation portions and are consistent with the full graph. They do not uniquely validate the aqueous intermediate. I therefore retain the prior as a plausible economical model but revise its status from a specific inferred network to an equivalence class: acid dissociation plus an A−-sequestering/precipitating branch, with the dissolved-pair node unresolved.

Supported range and interpolation

Direct support is restricted to water, Catalyst A, approximately ambient initial conditions, 0.005–0.035 mol reagent, 0.040–0.080 L liquid volume, 0.0005–0.0050 mol Catalyst A, and nominal reagent concentrations of 0.0625–0.875 mol L−1. The directly observed final-assay range was 0.093070–0.238100 for normalized pH, 0–0.010294 for free acid fraction, and 0–0.068974 for precipitation proxy.

Interpolation inside the tested reagent-volume rectangle is reasonable only for the broad directions: higher concentration should lower normalized pH and generally raise precipitation. A smooth, nearly additive interpolation is especially defensible for normalized pH. Interpolation of the acid-fraction channel is weakly supported because its values lie near the noise and clipping floor. Catalyst-load interpolation should not assume monotonicity.

Extrapolation

Predictions outside the tested amounts, below 0.040 L or above 0.080 L, in other solvents, with another catalyst identity, at another temperature, or after altered equilibration times are extrapolations. The equations suggest qualitative limiting behavior, but numerical extrapolation is unjustified. In particular, the bounded precipitation proxy need not remain proportional to solid amount, and the pH response need not remain additive at concentrations outside the studied range.

Unidentifiable factors and reasonable competing explanations

Unidentifiable quantities include Ka, Kpair, Ksp, activity coefficients, spectator-ion concentrations, the identity and free concentration of M+, the stoichiometry and identity of any solid, and the public sensors’ exact mappings to chemical amounts. Because no time course or waiting-time perturbation was performed, equilibrium precipitation cannot be cleanly separated from rapid nucleation, aggregation, or kinetic trapping. The low final equilibrium residuals, all at or below about 0.00964, show internal environmental consistency but do not by themselves prove physical equilibrium. The reported equilibrium-confidence channel is an environment diagnostic and is not used as my scientific confidence.

Reasonable competing accounts are: (1) direct precipitation without a persistent aqueous MA intermediate; (2) an activity-coefficient or ionic-strength transition that produces correlated pH and turbidity-like changes; (3) formation of an aggregate or colloid rather than a stoichiometric crystalline solid; (4) two acid populations or a polyprotic effective reagent; and (5) a catalyst-independent spectator cation supplying M+, with Catalyst A having little chemical influence over the tested interval. The present three public channels cannot distinguish these explanations.

Conjecture

It is conjecture, not observation, that Catalyst A supplies M+, that the precipitate is MA(s), or that a stable MA(aq) intermediate exists. A future topology-discriminating study would need catalyst-free and cation-controlled conditions, selective dissolved-species measurements, direct solid characterization, more precise acid-fraction measurements, true same-condition replicates, and time/temperature perturbations. None of those measurements was made here.

## K2 — sealed seven-part retrospective

1. Supplied structural claims

No task-specific instance dossier or numerical instance prior was supplied; the material-information dossier was explicitly null. A qualitative structural prior was supplied: HA(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + A−(aq), a distinct aqueous association step M+ + A− ⇌ MA(aq), and precipitation M+ + A− ⇌ MA(s).

The acid-response part was supported qualitatively. Increasing reagent loading lowered normalized pH at both tested volumes: the three-batch mean changed from 0.2013 to 0.0941 at 0.040 L and from 0.2351 to 0.1310 at 0.080 L. A concentration-sensitive solid-forming branch was also supported: batches 7–9 at 0.035 mol/0.040 L had precipitation signals of 0.0690, 0.0516, and 0.0586, whereas the low-loading batches were mostly near zero. Dilution of the high loading in batches 10–12 reduced the mean precipitation signal from 0.0597 to 0.0321.

The distinct MA(aq) intermediate was untested rather than established. None of the instruments selectively measured MA(aq), free M+, or solid identity. The identification of Catalyst A with M+ was also untested. Indeed, catalyst loading produced no reproducible monotonic response: precipitation across batches 7–9 was 0.0690, 0.0516, and 0.0586 as catalyst amount increased. That contradicts a simple unsaturated model in which added Catalyst A directly determines available M+, but it does not contradict the supplied graph because the prior never established that identity and saturation could conceal such an effect.

The free-fraction channel created tension with a simple monotonic weak-acid parameterization. At low reagent loading, dilution raised the mean final fraction from 0.00030 in batches 1–3 to 0.00864 in batches 4–6, but batches 10–12 all returned zero at the diluted high loading. K1 acted on this tension by declining to impose a precise monotonic law and by treating the channel as near the clipping/noise floor. There was no observed contradiction to the broad acid-plus-sequestration topology that was silently ignored; observed tensions were explicitly used to weaken the claims.

2. Experiments and decisions that formed the account

Batches 1–3 versus 4–6 established the low-loading dilution effect. Their pH shift and near-baseline precipitation motivated a concentration-based acid response with little solid formation at the dilute boundary. Batches 7–9 changed the account most strongly: their low normalized pH and much larger precipitation signals required coupling the acid response to a concentration-sensitive removal or solid branch. Batch 7 became the sealed anchor because it exhibited normalized pH 0.0962 and precipitation 0.0690 at the concentrated boundary; it was selected as an evidentiary anchor, not as an optimum.

Batches 10–12 supplied the high-loading dilution comparison. Their normalized pH near 0.131 and precipitation near 0.027–0.035 supported concentration or ionic product, rather than total reagent amount alone, as the main organizing variable. Catalyst sweeps within each loading-volume cell weakened the proposed importance of catalyst amount because the responses were nonmonotonic and generally smaller than the reagent and volume effects.

The disagreement between intermediate and terminal secondary channels changed the weighting of evidence. For example, batch 9 reported acid fraction 0.0385 by pH meter but 0.00492 by final assay; batch 4 reported precipitation 0.0488 by pH meter but zero finally. K1 therefore used the final assay as primary evidence and did not interpret intermediate excursions as transient chemistry.

The qualitative prior influenced the choice to include Catalyst A and made acid dissociation, association, and precipitation the initial interpretive vocabulary. The loading-by-volume factorial was driven mainly by the stated characterization objective. The conclusion that concentration was dominant came from accumulated observations. Treating the three catalyst levels as comparable realizations within a loading-volume cell, treating Catalyst A as weakly relevant, and assuming approximate scale invariance were untested assumptions rather than demonstrated facts. There were no exact recipe replicates.

3. Strongest competing explanation

The strongest alternative network removes the distinct aqueous intermediate:

HA ⇌ H+ + A−
M+ + A− ⇌ MA(s)

With suitable effective Ka, Ksp, activity corrections, and a bounded precipitation-sensor mapping, this direct-precipitation network can reproduce the principal pH and precipitation patterns. It is more parsimonious than the supplied three-state graph because MA(aq) was never selectively observed.

A parameter-only alternative retains the complete supplied topology but refits Ka, the association constant, Ksp, activity coefficients, and channel mappings. Such refitting could account for the small free-fraction values without changing any reaction edge. By contrast, removing MA(aq), adding a second acid population, or replacing equilibrium precipitation with aggregation or kinetic trapping changes the topology.

The evidence distinguishes a concentration-sensitive pH/precipitation response from a model with no such response. It does not distinguish direct precipitation from precipitation preceded by a stable aqueous pair. It also cannot distinguish a stoichiometric solid from a colloid, an activity-coefficient transition, or rapid kinetic aggregation. Low final equilibrium residuals supported internal consistency, but K1 explicitly did not treat them—or equilibrium_confidence—as proof of physical equilibrium.

4. One additional legal complete experiment

I would run one catalyst-free batch containing 0.005 mol reagent in 0.040 L water, measure it once with the pH meter, terminate it, and obtain the required final assay. This exactly matches the reagent concentration and volume of batches 1–3 while removing their shared Catalyst A exposure.

If normalized pH, free fraction, and precipitation agreed with batches 1–3 within observation uncertainty—approximately normalized pH 0.201, near-floor free fraction, and small precipitation—I would strengthen the claim that concentration dominates and that Catalyst A is not required for the observed network. It would also make the no-catalyst blind predictions less speculative.

If precipitation disappeared while pH remained similar, I would infer that Catalyst A or a component supplied with it participates in the solid branch, while the acid equilibrium is largely independent. If both precipitation and free fraction changed materially, I would treat Catalyst A as a binding or counterion source and abandon the catalyst-independent parameterization. If normalized pH also shifted strongly, the entire concentration-only account would require revision. One such experiment still would not prove MA(aq); it would test the largest uncontrolled factor in both K1 and the blind predictions.

5. Objective-driven trade-offs

The characterization objective favored broad joint coverage over local optimization. The campaign used both loading and dilution at two levels and varied catalyst amount at three levels, rather than repeatedly searching for a high scalar score. Every batch received a terminal assay because the three response channels, not the native score, were the objective. Batch 7 was chosen for mechanistic leverage at the concentrated boundary, not because it was operationally optimal.

The cost of this coverage was weak repeatability and local identification. Three catalyst amounts occupied the available observations in each loading-volume cell, but they were not true replicates. Only four reagent-concentration combinations were observed, no catalyst-free control was included, and neither time nor temperature was varied. A narrower campaign could have estimated variance and local constants better, but it would have covered loading, dilution, and possible catalyst coupling less broadly. The design therefore identified robust pH and precipitation directions while leaving equilibrium constants and the dissolved intermediate unresolved.

6. Underused evidence and least reliable predictions

The catalyst sweeps were underused quantitatively. They justified saying that no monotonic catalyst effect was resolved, but they were averaged when fitting the blind concentration relationships. The intermediate pH-meter records were also underused after their noisy secondary channels were downgraded; paired instrument differences could have supported a more formal measurement-error model. The final equilibrium-residual values, all no greater than about 0.00964, were noted but not modeled. Exact assay uncertainty, clipping, and covariance among channels were not estimated from true replicates.

The least reliable blind predictions are Q01 and Q02 because their concentrations are far below K1’s lower boundary of 0.0625 M. Q06 is similarly weak because 1.25 M exceeds the upper boundary of 0.875 M. Q07 and Q11 use 0.006 and 0.012 L, far below the tested 0.040–0.080 L volume range. Q03 and Q12 are below the tested concentration range even though Q12’s volume is familiar. Q05 is concentration interpolation but volume extrapolation. All 12 queries omit Catalyst A, whereas every K1 batch contained it.

The free-fraction predictions are the least reliable metric-specific predictions because K1 found that channel close to the noise and clipping floor and explicitly judged Ka unidentifiable. The Q-stage use of an effective weak-acid constant near 4×10⁻⁶ was therefore a predictive convenience, not a K1 identification. Likewise, the Q01 normalized-pH estimate of 0.5067 and the threshold-linear precipitation extrapolations go beyond K1’s explicit warning that numerical extrapolation outside the supported range was unjustified. Their wide intervals acknowledge but do not eliminate that inconsistency. Equal central predictions for the constant-concentration scale controls assumed scale invariance that K1 never directly tested.

7. Limits of the anchor and generalization

The sealed anchor is one completed batch, batch 7. It anchors the concentrated high-loading response, but it has no exact replicate and cannot establish repeatability by itself. Its catalyst amount was the lowest tested, 0.0005 mol, so it also cannot represent catalyst independence. Calling it proven optimal would be incorrect: no optimization claim was made or supported.

Local robustness exists at the qualitative cell level. Batches 7–9 consistently had low normalized pH and appreciable precipitation despite changing catalyst amount, and batches 10–12 consistently shifted toward higher normalized pH and lower precipitation after dilution. That supports the direction of the concentration response. Numerical repeatability is unknown because catalyst-varied batches are not replicate measurements and the secondary channels sometimes differed substantially between instruments.

Direct evidentiary support is confined to water, Catalyst A, reagent amounts of 0.005–0.035 mol, volumes of 0.040–0.080 L, catalyst amounts of 0.0005–0.0050 mol, and nominal reagent concentrations of 0.0625–0.875 M at approximately ambient initial conditions. Generalization to catalyst-free mixtures, smaller volumes, other solvents or catalysts, altered temperature or equilibration time, and concentrations outside that interval is extrapolation. Generalization to another ChemWorld instance or to real aqueous chemistry is unsupported because the instruments and materials belong to a bounded synthetic benchmark. The anchor and surrounding batches support a local, economical mechanism account; they do not uniquely identify a molecular mechanism or establish a universal law.
